# Administrative Writing — Advanced: Answer Key

**For instructor use only. Not for distribution to students.**

Model answers are provided for all exercises with specific expected responses. The Advanced course involves substantial analysis and creative writing; guidance notes are given for extended free-writing tasks (~150 words) and scenario-based tasks. Where the revised example text is already printed in the student book, it is referenced rather than reprinted.

# MODULE 1. Advanced Foundations for Administrative Writing (Units 1–3)

## Unit 1. Controlling Sentence Complexity

### A. Opening & Activation — Identifying Confusing Sentences

**Sentence 1:** *We confirmed the applicant submitted updated documents that were incomplete because the translation team had not finished their review although they intended to submit a draft earlier which caused delays.*

- **Main idea:** We confirmed that the documents were incomplete.
- **Secondary ideas:** reason (translation team hadn't finished), concession (they intended to submit earlier), consequence (delays).
- **Source of confusion:** Three relative/subordinate clauses are stacked without clear segmentation. The reader loses track of the main subject-verb by the end.

**Sentence 2:** *The meeting participants agreed to revise the guidelines which were noted by several offices to be inconsistent with previous instructions that require further study.*

- **Main idea:** Participants agreed to revise the guidelines.
- **Secondary ideas:** evaluation by offices (inconsistent with previous instructions), need for further study.
- **Source of confusion:** The relative clause "which were noted..." embeds a judgment inside the main decision. The phrase "that require further study" is ambiguous — does it refer to the guidelines or to the previous instructions?

**Sentence 3:** *Since the system update was completed last month but the manual was not updated, staff reported errors that made processing applications difficult which resulted in delays.*

- **Main idea:** Staff reported errors that caused delays.
- **Secondary ideas:** cause (system update without matching manual update), consequence (difficult processing, delays).
- **Source of confusion:** "Since... but..." combines a subordinator with a coordinator awkwardly. "Which resulted in delays" dangles ambiguously at the end.

---

### C. Language Focus — Add Coordinators

1. *We need to confirm the applicant's address,* **and** *we must also check the original application form.*
2. *The team attempted to submit the report,* **but** *the system outage prevented completion.*
3. *The deadline is approaching,* **so** *we appreciate your prompt cooperation.*
4. *The office received two versions of the document,* **and** *it is unclear which one should be processed.* / ...***yet*** *it is unclear...*

_Note: Items 1 and 4 may also accept "however" (with semicolon) where stronger contrast/addition is intended. Full credit for any grammatically correct and logically appropriate choice with explanation._

---

### C. Language Focus — Identify the Subordination Function

1. *Because the file was corrupted, the attachments did not open.* — **Reason**
2. *While we understand the concern, the process cannot be changed at this stage.* — **Contrast**
3. *When the update is complete, the system will prompt users to log in again.* — **Time**
4. *Although the instructions were revised, some teams continued using the previous version.* — **Contrast**

---

### C. Language Focus — Apply the Clarity Patterns

**1.** *Although we received the applicant's correction, we still need to confirm whether the previous version remains valid because the internal database has not been updated.* → **Segmentation:**
> We received the applicant's correction. However, we still need to confirm whether the previous version remains valid, as the internal database has not yet been updated.

**2.** *Since several offices raised concerns about the inconsistency, we will draft a revised explanation to ensure alignment.* → **Pattern 1 (main idea → reason):**
> We will draft a revised explanation to address the inconsistency raised by several offices. / We will issue a revised explanation because several offices raised concerns about the inconsistency.

**3.** *We must confirm whether the translation is final, and we must also check whether the correct version was submitted, and this is needed before the examination can proceed.* → **Segmentation:**
> We must confirm whether the translation is final and whether the correct version was submitted. Both points need to be resolved before the examination can proceed.

**4.** *The department will review the file once the updated form has been received and after the confirmation from the regional office has been processed.* → **Pattern 2 (purpose clause → main action):**
> Once the updated form has been received and the regional office's confirmation has been processed, the department will review the file. / *Alternatively:* To ensure the review can proceed, please note that both the updated form and regional office confirmation must be received first.

---

### G. Editing — Improve Clarity: Ambiguous Sentences

**1.** *We informed the office that the report was incorrect, which caused confusion.*

- **Problem:** "which" is ambiguous — it could refer to our informing them, or to the report being incorrect.
- **Revision:** We informed the office that the report was incorrect. This notification caused some confusion. / The office was confused when we notified them that the report contained an error.

**2.** *They updated the database after reviewing the submitted documents that contained several errors.*

- **Problem:** "that contained several errors" could modify "database" or "documents."
- **Revision:** After reviewing the submitted documents, which contained several errors, they updated the database. / They found several errors in the submitted documents and then updated the database accordingly.

**3.** *The revision was sent to the department because they requested clarification even though the previous version was approved.*

- **Problem:** "even though the previous version was approved" creates a logical contradiction — it is unclear whether the revision was sent despite or because of the approval.
- **Revision:** The department requested clarification, so the revision was sent to them. This was the case even though the previous version had already been approved. / Although the previous version was approved, the department requested clarification, and the revision was sent accordingly.

## Unit 2. Tone for Agency-Level Communication

### A. Opening — Compare Tone and Clarity (Example Text)

**Identify features in the revised text:**
- **Softening phrases:** "It appears that the submitted document may not match..." (*may not* + *appears*); "we would appreciate it if you could..."
- **Reason-giving:** "To ensure accuracy in our review..."
- **Shared-goal framing:** "we will be able to proceed with the next steps of the examination" (mutual benefit)
- **Removal of direct blame:** Original: "You submitted the wrong document." Revised: "the submitted document may not match" (passive + hedging removes the "you did X wrong" framing)

---

### C. Language Focus — Match Softening Phrase to Function

| Phrase | Function |
|--------|---------|
| A. *It appears that…* | 3. Introducing a possible issue without assigning blame |
| B. *We would appreciate it if you could…* | 2. Making an indirect request |
| C. *For our internal confirmation…* | 4. Clarifying the purpose or reason for a request |
| D. *To ensure accuracy…* | 1. Providing a neutral justification |

---

### C. Language Focus — Rewrite Direct Questions

1. *Why did you change the format?* → Could you clarify the reason for the recent format change? / We would like to understand the background to the format adjustment. Could you provide some context?

2. *When will you finish the revision?* → Could you advise on the expected timeline for the revision? / Would you be able to confirm when the revised version will be available?

3. *Who made this decision?* → Could you confirm which office or individual authorised this decision? / For our records, we would appreciate knowing the source of this decision.

4. *Did you read the previous instructions?* → For our confirmation, could you clarify whether the previous instructions were taken into account when preparing this document?

---

### C. Language Focus — Choose the Appropriate Version for Each Audience

| Audience | Version | Reasoning |
|----------|---------|-----------|
| A. Internal colleague (same office) | **Version 1**: *"Send the corrected file today."* | Direct instruction is acceptable internally; no need for diplomatic softening between same-office colleagues in routine contexts. |
| B. Domestic agency counterpart | **Version 2**: *"Could you kindly share the corrected file when available?"* | Polite modal + "when available" shows consideration; appropriate for established agency-to-agency relationships. |
| C. Foreign IP office | **Version 3**: *"We would appreciate receiving the updated file at your earliest convenience."* | Highest level of diplomacy; "we would appreciate" is more deferential; avoids any implication of pressure; appropriate for international correspondence. |

---

### G. Editing — Diplomatic Sentences

1. *You did not attach the file.* → It appears that the attachment may not have been included in the most recent message. Could you resend it at your convenience?
   - **Softening strategy:** *It appears that...* (removes direct accusation); modal hedge (*may not have been*)

2. *This mistake caused significant delay.* → The discrepancy in the submitted version has resulted in a delay to the review timeline.
   - **Softening strategy:** Passive voice (*has resulted in*); nominalization (*discrepancy* removes personal blame); *mistake* replaced by neutral *discrepancy*

3. *We already told you to revise this.* → As mentioned in our previous correspondence, this section requires revision before the file can proceed.
   - **Softening strategy:** *As mentioned in our previous correspondence* (neutral reference, not accusatory); *requires revision* instead of "you need to"

4. *Your clarification doesn't make sense.* → We appreciate the clarification provided. To ensure our understanding is accurate, may we ask one additional question regarding [specific point]?
   - **Softening strategy:** Opens with appreciation; reframes as a follow-up question rather than a rejection of the previous answer

## Unit 3. Cohesion & Logical Flow

### A. Opening — Classify Excerpts by Cohesion Type

| # | Type | Reason |
|---|------|--------|
| 1 | **Disjointed** | Five unrelated facts with no logical connection between them |
| 2 | **Cohesive** | Each sentence builds on the previous; connectors ("To address these") signal purpose |
| 3 | **Disjointed** | Four short declarative sentences with no connectors; topics shift abruptly |
| 4 | **Cohesive** | "Because" links cause to effect; second sentence shows result and outcome |
| 5 | **Disjointed** | Reads like a sequence of events but lacks connectors; "The team reviewed" to "Approval was given" jumps without explanation |
| 6 | **Cohesive** | "Several errors… returned for correction" → "once reviewed and approved… forwarded" — clear cause-and-effect chain |
| 7 | **Disjointed** | Three disconnected facts with no logical relationship shown |
| 8 | **Cohesive** | "Confirmed details" → "ready to proceed" → "in line with updated procedure" → "by Friday" — logical sequence with clear purpose |

---

### C. Language Focus — Sequence Sentences for Logical Flow

**Best order: B → C → A → D**

1. B: *Several agencies reported confusion about the updated guidelines.* _(introduces the problem)_
2. C: *To address these concerns, we agreed to prepare additional clarification.* _(decision/response)_
3. A: *We will draft a revised explanation to ensure consistency across offices.* _(action)_
4. D: *Once the revised explanation has been approved internally, it will be distributed to all relevant offices.* _(next step / outcome)_

---

### C. Language Focus — Replace Repetition with Reference Words

1. *The document contains errors. **It** must be corrected before submission.* / *The document contains errors and must be corrected before submission.*

2. *The applicants raised several questions. **These** will be addressed at the next meeting.*

3. *The office submitted the revision late. **This delay** caused problems for the review team.*

4. *The procedure was updated in January. **It** applies to all agencies from March onwards.*

---

### C. Language Focus — Add Logical Connectors

1. *The translation contains several inconsistencies.* **Therefore** / **As a result**, *we need to request clarification from the office.*

2. *The review was completed.* **However**, *two errors remain.*

3. *The instructions were updated.* **Nevertheless** / **However**, *some teams are still using the previous version.*

4. *The applicant submitted the required documents.* **However** / **Nevertheless**, *the file cannot be processed because the declaration form is missing.*

_Note: Accept any logically appropriate connector with a brief explanation. Full credit for justified choices with correct punctuation (semicolon + however / however as standalone)._

---

### D. Guided Writing — Reorganise Into a Coherent Two-Paragraph Explanation

**Information provided:** (see student book exercise)

**Model two-paragraph version:**

> The coordination meeting took place on 14 March, during which three key issues were identified. The issues related to terminology, submission formats, and internal deadlines. Offices were asked to respond by 20 March; however, confirmation has not yet been received from two of the participating offices.
>
> To address the outstanding concerns, the team agreed that updated guidelines should be circulated before the next meeting. A follow-up message will be sent by Friday to ensure all offices are informed and can respond before the planned deadline.

---

### G. Editing — Improve Paragraph Cohesion

**1.** *"The applicant submitted the revised form. The guidelines were updated. The team is reviewing the change. We will send a confirmation."*

**Revised:**
> The applicant submitted the revised form following the recent guidelines update. The team is currently reviewing the change to ensure it aligns with the new requirements. Once the review is complete, a confirmation will be sent.

_Changes: (1) Used "following" to connect sentences 1 and 2; (2) "to ensure" adds reason to sentence 3; (3) "Once... a confirmation will be sent" — passive + conditional for professional tone_

**2.** *"There were delays in the translation process. Some information was missing. The office did not receive the updated version. We must check which version is correct."*

**Revised:**
> The translation process was delayed, partly because some required information was missing. As a result, the office did not receive the updated version. We therefore need to clarify which version is correct before the review can continue.

_Changes: (1) "partly because" connects cause; (2) "As a result" signals consequence; (3) "therefore" shows logical follow-through_

# MODULE 2. Professional Email Writing for Interagency Contexts (Units 4–7)

## Unit 4. Structuring High-Level Emails

### A. Opening — Identify Communication Issues

**Problems with the weak email:**
1. No clear reason for writing (no opening statement of purpose)
2. Multiple unrelated requests mixed without separation
3. Informal opening ("Hi") inappropriate for interagency level
4. No reference to specific documents or file identifiers
5. "We need to finish the review soon" — vague urgency with no specific timeline

---

### B. Example Text — Compare Tone and Clarity

**Key differences (revised vs. original):**

| Feature | Original | Revised |
|---------|---------|---------|
| Greeting | "Hi" | Formal opening: "Thank you for your message" |
| Request | Vague, combined | Numbered, separated |
| References | None | "Section 3.1", "Annex A" |
| Next step | Absent | "Once confirmed, we will proceed..." |
| Tone | Informal, pressuring | Professional, diplomatic |

---

### C. Language Focus — Multi-Part Email Structure

**Model phrases for introducing multiple requests:**

- *We are writing to address two points arising from the submitted file...*
- *We have noted the following issues, which we would like to raise separately...*
- *Our message today relates to two matters. First,... Second,...*

---

### G. Editing — Restructure the Email

**Original:** "Hi, we saw your message. Can you send the other file? Also, the translation had some issues so please check that too. By the way, we need to finish the review soon."

**Model rewrite:**

> Subject: Follow-Up — Updated File and Translation Review
>
> Dear [Name],
>
> Thank you for your message.
>
> We are writing to follow up on two points. First, could you confirm whether the updated file mentioned in your message is now available? Second, we noted several inconsistencies in the translation submitted with the file. We would appreciate your review of those sections before the next stage proceeds.
>
> If possible, we would welcome a response by [date], as the review timeline depends on receiving both items.
>
> Kind regards,
> [Name]

---

## Unit 5. Writing Precise Clarification Requests

### A. Opening — Identify Communication Issues

**Problems with the weak request:**
1. "Some questions" — vague; no indication of how many or which type
2. "The parts that are unclear" — reader cannot identify which parts without more context
3. "Is the date correct?" — which date? Correct according to what reference?
4. No document reference; no next-step statement after confirmation

---

### C. Language Focus — Rewrite Requests for Precision

1. *Can you explain the date issue?* → In Section [X], the date listed appears to differ from [Annex / earlier version]. Could you confirm which date should be used?

2. *What does "response" mean here?* → In Section 2.3, the term "response" is used. Could you clarify whether this refers only to written responses, or whether it also includes verbal or electronic communications?

3. *Is this correct?* → Could you confirm whether the figure in [Section/Table X] is correct, as it appears to differ from the value stated in [Annex/page X]?

4. *We have questions about the guidelines.* → We have three questions regarding the revised guidelines. First,... Second,... Third,...
   _(Instruct students to complete with specific questions from their context.)_

5. *Please confirm the timeline.* → Could you confirm the intended timeline for [specific process]? In particular, we would like to clarify the deadline for [specific action], as mentioned in [Section/document].

---

### C. Language Focus — Phrase Bank Building

| Introducing clarification | Referencing documents |
|--------------------------|----------------------|
| For our confirmation, we would appreciate clarification on… | In Section 3.1 of the attached document… |
| To ensure accuracy, may we confirm whether… | According to the table in Annex B… |
| We would be grateful if you could clarify… | The term used on page 4… |
| It appears that… | There may be a discrepancy regarding… |

| Formulating questions | Next steps |
|----------------------|-----------|
| Could you confirm which version is correct? | Once confirmed, we will proceed with… |
| Could you specify which version is currently valid? | We will update our internal records once we receive your response. |
| Could you confirm whether X and Y refer to the same procedure? | |

## Unit 6. Communicating Errors Diplomatically

### G. Editing — Rewrite for Diplomatic Communication

_Typical exercise: Direct error reports → diplomatically rewritten versions._

**Model approach:**
- Replace "you made an error" with "it appears that there may be a discrepancy..."
- Replace "this was wrong" with "this may not match the required specification"
- Add reason-giving and a cooperative next step

**Example transformations:**

1. "The data you submitted contains errors." →
   > It appears that some data in the submitted file may not match our internal records. We would appreciate your confirmation before we proceed.

2. "You sent the wrong form." →
   > The form received may not be the most recent version. Could you confirm whether an updated version is available?

3. "You missed the deadline." →
   > We note that the submission was received after the indicated deadline. We would like to clarify how this should be handled going forward.

---

## Unit 7. Layered Requests: Managing Multi-Part Actions

### G. Editing — Restructure Multi-Part Requests

_Typical exercise: Unstructured multi-part requests → clearly sequenced versions._

**Model approach:**
- Separate each request into a numbered point
- State any dependencies clearly ("Before... please first...")
- Add a clear next-step statement

**Example transformations:**

1. "Please check the form, update the system, and tell us the result." →
   > We would appreciate your assistance with the following steps: (1) Please review the attached form for completeness. (2) Once reviewed, kindly update the record in the system. (3) Could you then confirm once both steps are complete?

# MODULE 3. Writing Complex Procedures & Internal Explanations (Units 8–11)

## Unit 8. Writing Clear Procedures

### G. Editing — Repair the Procedure

_Typical exercise: Repair a vague or incorrectly ordered procedure._

**Model approach:**
- Identify the correct logical sequence
- Ensure each step begins with a clear action verb
- Remove conditional logic from procedure steps (conditions belong in introductory text)
- Add a closing step that signals task completion

---

## Unit 9. Change Management Writing

### G. Editing — Repair the Change Communication

_Typical exercise: Repair an ineffective change announcement._

**Model approach:**
- State what is changing first (not background first)
- Add the reason for the change
- Specify the effective date and scope
- Confirm who needs to act and what action is required

---

## Unit 10. Explaining Complex Issues

### G. Editing — Repair the Problem Explanation

_Typical exercise: A vague or poorly structured problem explanation._

**Model approach:**
- Segment the explanation into: background → issue → cause → effect → status
- Replace passive vague language with specific facts
- Ensure the final sentence leads into the next action or outcome

---

## Unit 11. Executive-Level Meeting Summaries

### G. Editing — Repair the Meeting Summary

_Typical exercise: A weak or disorganised summary._

**Model approach:**
- Open with the meeting purpose and date
- Present main points in logical order (discussion → decision → action)
- Separate decisions from general discussion
- End with clear action items, ownership, and timing

# MODULE 4. Cross-Border & Interagency Communication (Units 12–15)

## Unit 12. High-Level External Inquiries

### C. Language Focus — Key features of a strong external inquiry at advanced level:
- Full professional opening with relevant context (not just "I have a question")
- Specific document or section references
- Diplomatically phrased questions (modal + specific scope)
- Statement of why the clarification matters (impact on decision/process)
- Clear next-step statement

### G. Editing

_Typical exercise: Upgrade a basic external inquiry to advanced level._

**Model approach:**
- Add interagency context and document references
- Structure questions as numbered points
- Use advanced softening strategies from Unit 2
- Close with a statement of how the reply will be used

---

## Unit 13. Writing for Risk & Impact Assessment

### G. Editing — Strengthen a Risk/Impact Assessment Paragraph

**Model approach:**
- State the risk or finding neutrally (not alarmist, not dismissive)
- Identify the scope and scale (which process/office/timeline is affected)
- Present impact using conditional framing ("If unaddressed, this may...")
- Suggest mitigation or next step without over-committing
- Maintain consistent formal register throughout

---

## Unit 14. Writing Complex Multi-Audience Messages

### C. Key principle — One situation, multiple versions:

The same factual information requires different framing for:
- **Internal audiences:** direct, concise, action-focused
- **Interagency:** neutral, context-providing, collaborative framing
- **International/external:** diplomatic, formally softened, next-step explicit

### G. Editing

_Typical exercise: Identify audience mismatch in a draft → revise._

**Model approach:**
- Identify who the intended reader is
- Check whether the tone (directness, formality, softening) matches that reader
- Revise phrasing without changing the core facts

---

## Unit 15. Drafting Policy-Aligned Rationales

### G. Editing — Strengthen the Rationale

**Model approach:**
- Open with the policy or principle being applied
- Link the specific decision to that principle clearly
- Provide brief factual justification
- Close with the effect of this alignment on the current process/decision

# MODULE 5. Advanced Editing & Revision Skills (Units 16–18)

## Unit 16. Writing Balanced Evaluations

### C. Key structure for a balanced evaluation:
1. State the subject and context
2. Present strengths (specific, evidence-based)
3. Present weaknesses or gaps (neutral language, not accusatory)
4. Conclude with an overall assessment or recommendation

### G. Editing — Repair a One-Sided Evaluation

**Model approach:**
- Ensure both strengths and limitations are acknowledged
- Replace strong positive/negative judgments with measured assessments
- Use hedged language for limitations: "may benefit from...", "could be strengthened by..."

---

## Unit 17. Drafting Executive Briefs

### G. Editing — Repair the Executive Brief

_Typical exercise: A verbose or poorly structured brief._

**Model approach:**
- Keep each section to its minimum functional length (context = 2 sentences maximum)
- Ensure the recommended action or decision is explicit and early
- Remove supporting detail that is not essential to the decision
- Use headings or clear paragraph breaks

---

## Unit 18. Synthesising Information from Multiple Sources

### G. Editing — Repair the Synthesis

_Typical exercise: Disconnected paraphrases from multiple sources._

**Model approach:**
- Identify the common thread across sources and state it as the topic sentence
- Connect each source contribution with a transition that shows agreement, contrast, or addition
- End with an overall conclusion or implication, not just a final source paraphrase

# MODULE 6. Capstone & Applied Administrative Writing (Units 19–23)

## Units 19–23: Capstone and Applied Tasks

_These units use authentic-style scenarios requiring integrated use of all earlier skills. No single model answer applies._

**Instructor assessment framework:**

### For extended emails and memos:
- **Structure:** Is the message logically organised with a clear opening, body, and closing?
- **Tone:** Is the level of formality appropriate for the stated audience (internal / interagency / international)?
- **Precision:** Are requests, references, and terms specific?
- **Diplomatic framing:** Are problems stated without assigning blame?
- **Coherence:** Does the text flow logically between sentences and paragraphs?
- **Concision:** Is the message as short as it needs to be while remaining complete?

### For analytical tasks (evaluation, assessment, brief):
- **Balance:** Are all relevant perspectives represented?
- **Evidence:** Are claims supported by specific reference to the scenario?
- **Structure:** Does the text follow the appropriate format for the document type?
- **Register:** Is the language consistently formal and appropriate?

## Common Student Errors by Module

| Module | Common Error | Correction |
|--------|-------------|-----------|
| 1 | Treating segmentation as "making sentences shorter" | Segmentation is about separating logical jobs, not minimising length |
| 1 | Using "although" for equal contrast | Use "although" only when the contrast idea is secondary; use "but" or "however" for equal contrast |
| 2 | Softening to the point of vagueness | Softening must preserve clarity — the reader must still understand the purpose |
| 2 | Using the same level of formality for all audiences | Tone must vary; internal ≠ interagency ≠ foreign office |
| 3 | Adding connectors without changing sentence structure | Cohesion requires structural revision, not just connector insertion |
| 4–7 | Leaving next-step statements implicit | High-level emails must always state what happens after the reply is received |
| 8–11 | Passive-heavy procedures | Procedures should use active imperative verbs |
| 12–15 | Background before purpose in external inquiries | Start with purpose and context; do not bury the question at the end |
| 16–18 | Evaluations that only list strengths or weaknesses | All evaluations must be balanced and evidence-based |
