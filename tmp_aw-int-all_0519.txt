  Module Guide

Module 1 builds the first control layer for the whole book. The goal is not only to write simple
English, but to write English that is easy for a workplace reader to process quickly. Each unit
therefore asks you to notice the difference between a weaker version and a stronger version,
explain why the stronger version helps the reader, and revise your own writing before mov-
ing on.

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

    1.    write short sentences with a clear main message
    2.    choose tone that sounds polite and professional
    3.    organise one paragraph so the reader can follow the idea from start to finish

Use this module in the same way each time:

    1.    read the example comparison and notice what changed
    2.    underline the explanation about why the stronger version works
    3.    complete the self-check before freer practice
    4.    use the editing practice to repair common reader problems
    5.    finish the transfer extension so the skill moves beyond one exercise type

A. WARM-UP: WHAT MAKES A SENTENCE CLEAR?
             Sentence Comparison
Read the pairs of sentences. Choose the one that feels clearer.

    1.
             1.   The document was, after some discussion in the meeting yesterday, finally checked
                  by Mr. Sato.
             2.   Mr. Sato finally checked the document after the meeting yesterday.
    2.
             1.   It is requested that you will kindly send, as soon as it is convenient, the missing
                  form.
             2.   Please send the missing form as soon as possible.
    3.
             1.   The application which was submitted by the applicant last week has some missing
                  information.
             2.   The application submitted last week has some missing information.

Discussion: Why are the (b) sentences easier to read?

             Quick Check for a Clear Sentence
    1.   Put the main subject near the beginning.
    2.   Use one clear main verb.
    3.   Put the most important information in the main sentence, not in a long extra phrase.
    4.   Remove words that do not change the meaning.
B. EXAMPLE CHECK: WHY THE STRONGER SENTENCE WORKS
              Why the Stronger Sentence Works
Look again at the three pairs in the warm-up.

The clearer sentence usually improves one or more of these points:

    1.   the main subject appears earlier
    2.   the main action is expressed by a simple verb
    3.   extra information is kept shorter
    4.   the reader can understand the message in one reading

Compare Pair 2 again:

    1.   Weak: It is requested that you will kindly send, as soon as it is convenient, the missing form.
    2.   Stronger: Please send the missing form as soon as possible.

The stronger version works because the reader can see the action immediately:

    1.   send
    2.   the missing form
    3.   as soon as possible

The weaker version makes the reader wait too long before the real action appears.



C. LANGUAGE 1 - BASIC SENTENCE PATTERN (SVO / SVC)
                Basic Sentence Pattern
Most clear administrative English sentences follow this simple pattern:

                Subject + Verb + Object/Complement


Examples:

    1.   We (S) received (V) your application (O).
    2.   The applicant (S) submitted (V) the missing form (O).
    3.   Our office (S) is located in (V) Tokyo (C).
    4.   The system (S) is (V) busy (C).
    5.   The documents (S) are (V) ready (C).

             Practice - Identify the Parts
Identify the parts of each sentence using these actions:

    1.   Underline the subject
    2.   Circle the verb
    3.   Highlight the object or complement
    1.   Our office received your email yesterday.
    2.   The applicant submitted the additional documents.
    3.   We will check the application today.
    4.   The system shows an error message.
    5.   The examiner requested more information.

           Practice - Build Clear Sentences
Rewrite the word groups as clear SVO or SVC sentences.

    1.   (application / our office / receive / yesterday)
    2.   (examiner / request / more information)
    3.   (we / send / confirmation email / tomorrow)
    4.   (applicant / not / submit / fee / yet)
    5.   (system / show / error message / this morning)



C. LANGUAGE 2 - AVOIDING UNNECESSARY COMPLEXITY
             Simplification Strategies
Administrative English should be short, direct, and clear.

Three common ways to simplify a sentence:

    1.   change long formal openings to direct verbs
    2.   cut extra explanation that the reader does not need
    3.   move the main action closer to the subject

             Why This Works
Administrative readers often need to understand:

    1.   what happened
    2.   what is needed
    3.   what happens next

They should not have to search through a long sentence to find the main point.

When you simplify a sentence, you are not making it “less professional.” You are making it
more useful for the reader.

             Simplify the Sentence
Rewrite each sentence in a clearer, shorter way.
    1.   Original: With reference to your email which was sent to our section yesterday afternoon, we
         are now in the process of checking the details. Clear:
    2.   Original: It has been decided by our department that the deadline for submission of the
         documents will be extended until 30 June. Clear:
    3.   Original: Due to the fact that the system is currently under maintenance, it is not possible for
         us to access your file at this moment in time. Clear:
    4.   Original: In the situation where the applicant does not provide the required documents, we
         will not be able to continue the examination. Clear:

B. EXAMPLE CHECK: WHY THE STRONGER TONE WORKS
             Why the Stronger Tone Works
Look again at this pair:

    1.   Weak: I don’t understand this. Explain it.
    2.   Stronger: I’m not sure I understand this part. Could you explain it?

The stronger version works because it:

    1.   reduces pressure on the reader
    2.   sounds cooperative, not demanding
    3.   keeps the focus on the task, not on blame

Professional tone does not mean “very formal words only.” It means the reader can cooperate
without feeling attacked.

C. LANGUAGE 2 - PROFESSIONAL TONE IN EXPLANATIONS
             Professional Tone in Explanations
Professional tone is:

    1.   Clear (not too long or complicated)
    2.   Neutral (not emotional)
    3.   Respectful (polite and calm)

When you check tone, ask:

    1.   Does this sound calm?
    2.   Does it tell the reader what they need to know?
    3.   Would I be comfortable sending this at work?

             Why This Works
A professional message helps the reader do the next step calmly.

If a message sounds too direct, the reader may focus on the tone instead of the task.

If a message sounds calm and clear, the reader can focus on:

    1.   what is needed
    2.   what needs clarification
    3.   what happens next

A. WHAT IS A PARAGRAPH?
             What Is a Paragraph?
A paragraph is a group of sentences about one main idea. All sentences in the paragraph
should connect to that idea.

You can think of a paragraph as answering one simple question such as:

    1.   What happened?
    2.   What is the problem?
    3.   What happens next?

A paragraph should

    1.   Have one clear topic
    2.   Contain supporting information about that topic
    3.   Include a closing sentence or a final point

A paragraph is NOT

    1.   A list of unrelated sentences
    2.   A collection of short, disconnected statements
    3.   A single sentence (except in special cases)



A. WHEN SHOULD YOU START A NEW PARAGRAPH?
             When to Start a New Paragraph
Start a new paragraph when:

    1.   The topic changes
    2.   The purpose of the message changes
    3.   You move from background to action, or reason to result
    4.   You begin addressing a different reader (less common in administrative writing)

Examples of paragraph breaks

    1.   Explaining the background ->Asking for an action
    2.   Describing an issue ->Giving details ->Explaining next steps
    3.   Summarising a problem ->Reporting what you did ->What will happen next



A. WARM-UP: WHICH IS A BETTER PARAGRAPH?
              Which Is a Better Paragraph?
Read both paragraphs. Decide which one is better organised.

Paragraph A

that were unclear and inconsistent with earlier versions. These inconsistencies may cause
confusion for staff who use the document. Additional notes will be compiled before we con-
tinue with the next stage of the review.

Paragraph B

Several sections in the document were unclear. Our team attended a training session last
week.

Discussion: Why is Paragraph A better?



B. EXAMPLE CHECK: WHY THE STRONGER PARAGRAPH
WORKS
              Why the Stronger Paragraph Works
Paragraph A is stronger because all sentences support one main topic: the review of the
guidelines.

Paragraph B is weaker because the reader has to jump between different topics:

    1.   guidelines
    2.   office renovation
    3.   unclear sections
    4.   training session

That makes the paragraph feel like a list, not a piece of organized writing.
              Why This Works
A good paragraph helps the reader move through one idea step by step.

In administrative writing, this matters because the reader often needs to know:

    1.   what the topic is
    2.   what details matter
    3.   what the result or next point is



C. LANGUAGE 1 - THREE-PART STRUCTURE
               Three-Part Paragraph Structure
A strong paragraph usually includes:

    1.   Topic sentence - the main idea of the paragraph
    2.   Supporting sentences - details, reasons, or examples
    3.   Closing sentence - summary, result, or next step

Example Paragraph
Topic sentence: We reviewed the applicant’s file yesterday. Supporting sentences: During
the review, we found that two pages were missing, and one form was incomplete. These gaps
in the file may slow the preliminary assessment and will need to be noted before the next
review stage. Closing sentence: This paragraph summarises the issues identified so far and
leads into further discussion of required documentation in the next section.

C. LANGUAGE 2 - LOGICAL FLOW INSIDE A PARAGRAPH
              Logical Flow Inside a Paragraph
A paragraph should follow a clear, logical order:

    1.   Background ->Details ->Result
    2.   Topic ->Explanation ->Action
    3.   Issue ->Impact ->What happens next

Before you write, make a short paragraph plan:

    1.   What is my main point?
    2.   What detail or explanation does the reader need?
    3.   How will I close the paragraph?

              Transfer Reminder

    1.   explaining a problem
    2.   giving background
    3.   summarising a result
    4.   preparing the next section

MODULE 1 STRATEGY MAP
             Module 1 Strategy Map
Use this map when a piece of writing feels weak but you are not sure why.

    1.   If the reader gets lost inside one sentence, return to Unit 1 and check the main subject, verb,
         and extra information.
    2.   If the message sounds too sharp, too casual, or too heavy, return to Unit 2 and check the
         relationship with the reader.
    3.   If the message feels complete sentence by sentence but still does not flow, return to Unit 3
         and check paragraph structure.

This matters because weak writing often has more than one problem at the same time. A
sentence can be grammatically correct but still sound too direct. A polite message can still
fail if the paragraph order is unclear. Strong administrative writing starts with control at all
three levels: sentence, tone, and paragraph.

A. WHAT DOES A PROFESSIONAL EMAIL LOOK LIKE?
               Basic Email Layout
A professional email has a clear structure so that the reader can easily understand the pur-

Basic Email Layout

      1.   Subject line - a short summary of the purpose
      2.   Greeting - polite opening
      3.   Opening sentence - main purpose of the email

C. LANGUAGE 2 - LOGICAL ORDER FOR UPDATE MESSAGES
              Logical Order for Update Messages
A clear update message usually follows this pattern:

    1.   Topic: What is delayed or changed

    4.   Next step / What will happen later: Neutral, not a request

              Why This Works
The paragraph moves in a useful order:

    1.   topic
    2.   reason
    3.   effect
    4.   next step

This order helps the reader understand both the situation and the practical meaning of the
delay.

C. LANGUAGE 1 - LEVELS OF POLITENESS IN REQUESTS
Different request forms sound more or less polite. In administrative writing, the level of
politeness depends on:

    1.   your relationship with the reader
    2.   the importance or urgency of the request
    3.   the formality of the situation

               Politeness Scale (From Direct to Most Polite)
Form | Example | Tone/Use |

|——|———|———–|

Can you…? | Can you send the file? | Casual, spoken English; too direct for administrative
emails. |

Could you…? | Could you send the file? | Standard polite request; appropriate for most sit-
uations. |

Could you please…? | Could you please send the file? | Polite and slightly more formal. |

Would you be able to…? | Would you be able to send the file? | Polite, softer, useful when
asking for something more difficult. |

Would it be possible to…? | Would it be possible to send the file? | Formal, respectful, avoids
pressure. |

I would appreciate it if you could… | I would appreciate it if you could send the file. | Very
polite, used when the request requires effort or cooperation. |

              Key Notes
    1.   Can you...? is grammatically correct but not appropriate** in most administrative emails.
    2.   **Could you...? is the safest default.
    3.   **Would you be able to...? softens the request and reduces pressure.
    4.   **I would appreciate it if... shows gratitude in advance and sounds formal.

               Adding Context (Why You Need the Information)
Often a request is clearer when you add one short context sentence.

    1.   We need this for the next review stage.
    2.   This information is required for the submission.
    3.   This will help us complete the process.

A. WHAT IS A PROCEDURE?
             What Is a Procedure?
A procedure is a set of steps that explains how to complete a task. It should:

    1.   Be clear and easy to follow
    2.   Use simple, direct language
    3.   Present steps in a logical order
    4.   Use consistent formatting (numbers or bullets)

Example (Part of a Larger Manual)
Steps to create a new file record:

    1.   Open the database system.
    2.   Select “New Record” from the main menu.
    3.   Enter the applicant’s basic information.
    4.   Save the record and confirm the entry.

    3.   Send the form to the next department.

A. WHAT IS A SIMPLE EXPLANATION?
             What Is a Simple Explanation?
A simple explanation:

    1.   Gives the reader background
    2.   States what is happening or what changed
    3.   Provides a reason, if appropriate
    4.   Avoids unnecessary technical detail
    5.   Uses neutral, professional language

checks. These checks are necessary to confirm applicant information. A further update will
be provided next week.

             Warm-Up: What Information Is Missing?

    1.   “The schedule changed.”
    2.   “The form is incorrect.”
    3.   “The applicant information is different.”
    4.   “The process will take longer.”

What is missing? 1. 2. 3. 4.
             A Useful Explanation Helps the Reader
    1.   understand the situation
    2.   see the reason or cause
    3.   understand the effect on the work



B. EXAMPLE CHECK: WHY A BETTER EXPLANATION WORKS
             Example Check: Why a Better Explanation Works
Compare:

    1.   Weak: The process will take longer.
    2.   Stronger: The process will take longer because two documents require additional checking. This
         means the next review stage will begin later than planned.

The stronger version works because it gives:

    1.   the situation
    2.   the reason
    3.   the effect

The reader does not need to guess the practical meaning.



C. LANGUAGE 1 - USEFUL PHRASES FOR EXPLANATIONS
               Language 1 - Useful Phrases for Explanations

    2.   This procedure is used when…
    3.   This step is required because…

Explaining the Reason

    1.   because…
    2.   due to…
    3.   as a result of…

Giving Additional Information

    1.   This means that…
    2.   In this case…
    3.   For this reason…
Mini explanation example:

             Why This Works
Simple explanations are useful when they help the reader follow a short chain:

background -> reason -> effect

If one part is missing, the explanation may sound incomplete even when the grammar is cor-

A. WHAT DOES CLARITY MEAN IN WRITING?
             What Does “Clarity” Mean in Writing?
Clarity refers to how easily the reader can understand your message. Clear writing:

    1.   Uses simple, direct language
    2.   Avoids long or complicated sentences
    3.   Presents ideas in a logical order
    4.   Avoids ambiguity or vague language

Example (Before -> After)

Before: The document, which was previously reviewed and looked at by two people, seems
like it maybe has some missing parts. After: The document appears to be missing some parts.

    3.   put related ideas together

Tone is how your writing sounds to the reader. A professional tone is:

    4.   Respectful

A poor tone may be:

    2.   Emotional
    3.   Impolite or demanding

    3.   “Fix this immediately.”

    2.   Stronger: Thank you for your message. The attached file appears to be a different version. Could you

    1.   removes blame
    2.   keeps the focus on the file, not the person
    3.   still asks clearly for the needed action

MODULE 5 EDITING-DECISION MAP
               Module 5 Editing-Decision Map
Module 5 is not only about finding errors. It is about deciding what kind of revision the text
needs first.

Use this order when you edit:

    1.   accuracy:
               1.    Is anything simply wrong?
    2.   clarity:
               1.    Can the reader follow the message without extra effort?
    3.   tone:
               1.    Will the reader react well enough to continue working with you?

This order helps because some tone problems disappear after clarity improves, and some
clarity problems disappear after an accuracy fix. Good editing is not random correction. It is
a controlled sequence.

MODULE 6 PORTFOLIO COMPLETION LADDER
                  Module 6 Portfolio Completion Ladder
Before final submission, move one portfolio item through this ladder:

    1.   Draft stage
             1.     identify the reader and purpose
    2.   Control stage
             1.     mark the main sentence, supporting detail, and next-step line
    3.   Revision stage
             1.     improve accuracy, clarity, and tone
    4.   Portfolio stage
             1.     compare the item to the other three pieces
             2.     decide what skill range it adds to the set
    5.   Final reflection stage
             1.     explain one revision decision that made the document more useful

Use these prompts:

    1.   Which portfolio item is the strongest example for audience control?
    2.   Which item still needs the clearest next-step line?
    3.   Which item benefits most from a Module 5 editing pass?
    4.   Which item best proves that the portfolio is a set of deliberate revisions rather than a set of
         disconnected exercises?

This ladder makes the capstone more teachable because it shows how one document moves
from draft to finished portfolio evidence. Without that bridge, the final portfolio can still feel
like a collection task instead of the end of a developed writing course.
