# Phase 5 Structural Element Terminology Consistency Audit

Date: 2026-06-10

Scope:
- `int/md/working/aw-int-all_0519.md`
- support guides consulted for context:
  - `int/edits & guides/planning/guide-set/aw-int_activity_framework.md`
  - `adv/edits & guides/planning/guide-set/aw-master_activity_menu.md`
  - `int/edits & guides/full-review/aw-int_print_readiness_repair_plan_0530.md`

Purpose:
- check whether the book uses consistent terminology when referring to the same structural element of a piece of writing
- identify places where students may be confused because one label is used for different parts, or different labels are used for the same part

## Proposed Standard Terminology

### Email

- `subject line` = the line naming the topic before the email body
- `greeting` = the salutation line, for example `Dear Ms. Kim,`
- `opening sentence` = the first full sentence that states the purpose
- `body` = the main content after the opening sentence
- `request / question / detail` = the functional content inside the body when a task needs a narrower name than `body`
- `closing sentence` = the polite final sentence before the sign-off
- `sign-off` = the final name/ending line, for example `Kind regards,`

Rule:
- do not use `opening` by itself when the real meaning is `greeting`
- do not group greetings and purpose sentences under the same label unless the book explicitly says it is grouping two different parts

### Paragraph

- `topic sentence`
- `supporting detail(s)` or `supporting sentence(s)`
- `closing sentence`

This set is mostly consistent already.

### Notice

- `heading`
- `background`
- `essential information`
- `next step`

This set is also mostly consistent already.

### Procedure

- `opening sentence` only when it means the sentence that explains who the procedure is for or what task it covers
- `step(s)`
- `closing sentence` only when it means the sentence explaining what happens after the task

## Main Findings

### 1. `opening` is used for different email parts

This is the main book-wide problem.

In some places, `opening` means the greeting:
- Unit 4 focus says `appropriate opening and closing`
- Unit 4 C says `Email Openings and Closings`
- Unit 15 C table uses `Opening` for items including `Thank you for your email.` and `I am writing to...`
- Unit 21 C uses `Standard Openings` for a mixed set of greeting lines and purpose lines

In other places, the book distinguishes `greeting` from `opening sentence`:
- Unit 4 B says the strong email has `the greeting is professional` and `the first sentence gives the reason for writing`
- Unit 4 C says `Opening Purpose Phrases`
- Unit 4 C says `Opening Sentence Job`
- Unit 4 F says `subject line, greeting, opening sentence, body, closing sentence, and sign-off`
- Unit 12 C says `subject line`, `greeting`, `background`, `inquiry question`, `closing sentence`, `sign-off`

Conclusion:
- the book has two competing systems:
  - `opening` as a broad umbrella term
  - `greeting` plus `opening sentence` as separate structural parts
- the second system is clearer and should be treated as the standard

### 2. Some phrase banks mix different structural parts under one heading

#### Unit 4

Location:
- around lines `1150-1183`

Problem:
- `Email Openings and Closings` includes:
  - `Dear Mr./Ms. [Name],`
  - `Dear Team,`
  - `Thank you for your email.`
- the first two are greetings
- `Thank you for your email.` is not a greeting in the same sense; it is an opening sentence or opening-purpose move

Effect:
- students may think `Dear Team,` and `Thank you for your email.` are the same kind of part
- this conflicts with the later explanation that the `opening sentence` tells the reader why they are writing

#### Unit 21

Location:
- around lines `6381-6399`

Problem:
- `Standard Openings` includes:
  - `Dear [Office/Team],`
  - `Thank you for your message.`
  - `We would like to request...`
  - `We have a question regarding...`
- this groups at least three different things:
  - greeting
  - opening sentence acknowledging a previous message
  - purpose sentence

Effect:
- students cannot tell whether `opening` means a greeting line or the first content sentence
- this undermines the earlier more precise six-part email structure

### 3. The book sometimes uses `opening sentence`, `purpose sentence`, and `first sentence` for the same email part

Examples:
- Unit 4 B: `the first sentence gives the reason for writing`
- Unit 4 C: `Opening Sentence Job`
- Unit 4 H: `a clear opening sentence`
- Module 2 review: `Does the opening sentence show the purpose early?`
- Unit 15: `purpose sentence`
- Unit 15 B: `complete purpose sentence`
- Unit 15 E: `purpose sentence, body, request or next step, and closing`

Assessment:
- these are close enough in meaning that students can probably follow them
- however, they should still be standardised

Recommendation:
- use `opening sentence` as the main structural term
- allow `purpose sentence` only when the book explicitly wants to emphasise function, not part-name
- avoid switching between `first sentence`, `opening sentence`, and `purpose sentence` without reason

### 4. `closing` and `closing sentence` are mostly stable, but not fully controlled

Examples:
- Unit 4 C: `Common Closings`
- Unit 4 B: `the closing sentence and sign-off are polite`
- Unit 12 C: `closing sentence`
- Unit 15 C: `Closing`
- Unit 21 C: `Standard Closings`

Assessment:
- this area is less serious than the `opening` problem
- the main issue is parallelism:
  - if the book distinguishes `greeting` from `opening sentence`, it should also distinguish `closing sentence` from `sign-off`

Recommendation:
- prefer `closing sentence` when teaching the structural part
- use `common closing phrases` or `common closings` only for phrase-bank labels
- keep `sign-off` separate

### 5. Paragraph terminology is comparatively strong

Relevant locations:
- Unit 3 around lines `645-843`

Observed pattern:
- `topic sentence`
- `supporting detail(s)` / `supporting sentence(s)`
- `closing sentence`

Assessment:
- this terminology is internally coherent
- the book uses both `supporting detail` and `supporting sentence`, but this does not create the same level of confusion as the email `opening` issue because both point to the same sentence-level support role in context

### 6. Notice terminology is comparatively strong

Relevant locations:
- Unit 9 around lines `2716-2745`

Observed pattern:
- `heading`
- `background`
- `essential information`
- `next step`

Assessment:
- this system is clear and function-based
- it is a good model for the email terminology problem because each label refers to one distinct structural job

## High-Priority Repair Targets

1. Unit 4 C `Email Openings and Closings`
   - highest confusion risk because it introduces email-element terminology early in the email module

2. Unit 15 C consistency language
   - this unit is explicitly about standardising email parts, so mixed terminology here is especially damaging

3. Unit 21 C `Standard Openings`
   - currently groups greeting lines and purpose lines together

## Recommended Next Step

Revise the manuscript so that all email-structure references use one consistent part vocabulary:

- `subject line`
- `greeting`
- `opening sentence`
- `body`
- `closing sentence`
- `sign-off`

Then update phrase-bank titles so they match the same system:

- `Common Greetings`
- `Common Closing Sentences` or `Common Closings`
- `Opening Purpose Phrases`

Do not leave `opening` as a standalone category label where it can mean either `greeting` or `opening sentence`.
